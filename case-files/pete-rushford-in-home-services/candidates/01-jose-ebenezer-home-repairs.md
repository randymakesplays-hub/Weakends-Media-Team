## Diagnosis

**He is the right *type* of person.** Pete asked for independent contractors with their
own thing going who want overflow to fill gaps — not employees, not people dependent on
him. Jose is that exactly: a decade solo, a two-person operation with his wife, an active
customer base, and no interest in being staffed. His first question on the cold call was
*"No lead fees?"* — he is protecting his own economics, which is the correct instinct and
a good sign.

**But his center of gravity is not Pete's.** Pete's bread and butter is the $150–400
same-day call-out — outlets, disposals, drains, dishwashers — two to four a day at roughly
a grand. Jose's stated strengths are painting and $3,000–3,500 bathroom renovations. That
is the *remodeling* work Pete deliberately walked away from because *"it's too
time-consuming and the payout is not near as good as daily call-outs."*

Read that two ways:

1. **Risk.** If Jose is slotted straight into the daily call-out queue, he is being used
   below his strength, and the arrangement gets boring or unprofitable for him fast.
2. **Bigger opportunity.** Pete is currently *turning down* remodel work. Jose is the
   natural home for it. That is not the brief Pete gave, but it is a second revenue stream
   Pete is actively discarding — worth putting to him on Friday.

**The weekend constraint is real.** Pete's clients are absentee landlords whose tenants
have emergencies. Pete answers personally for anything a sub touches. A Mon–Fri-only sub
is fine for scheduled make-readies and maintenance; it is a problem if Jose is treated as
on-call. Set the expectation explicitly rather than discovering it on a Saturday.

## Risks

1. **Identity is not nailed down.** The CRM says "Ebenezer Home Repairs." He answers the
   phone *"Yeah, DC Homes."* Randy called him **Ulises** across two calls before being
   corrected on the third — *"You got the wrong name… my name is Jose."* No last name was
   ever captured. Before Pete sees this file: confirm legal name, legal entity name, and
   which business the email address belongs to. Handing Pete a candidate whose name the
   recruiter got wrong twice undercuts the whole pitch.
2. **Four screening questions were never asked** — insurance, tools, vehicle, references.
   Insurance is the one Pete named as a requirement, and it is the one that costs him if
   it is missing. These must be closed *before* the three-way, not during it.
3. **No rate mapping exists.** Pete's reference point is subs taking home **$400–500/day**.
   Jose quotes per-project labor and won't price without seeing the work. Those two models
   don't meet on their own. Establish either a day rate, a per-call-out rate, or a split
   percentage before the three-way call, or that call becomes a negotiation instead of an
   introduction.
4. **Randy described a payment structure that doesn't exist yet.** On call 3 he laid out
   Pete holding the leads, handling communication, collecting the deposit, taking the
   larger percentage, pulling permits, and Jose being paid same-day — prefaced with *"what
   I imagine."* Jose heard a proposal. Pete has never agreed to any of it. Align with Pete
   first, or the three-way opens with a contradiction.
   *Credit where due:* Randy did commit to a written agreement — *"we don't do things word
   of mouth"* — which is the right answer and the opposite of where the Pete engagement
   itself currently stands.
5. **The screening call ended mid-question** and was never resumed. Randy said *"I'll give
   you a call back in like five minutes"*; the wrap-up text went out at 8:22 AM instead.
   The unanswered half was Jose's own question about how he gets paid.
6. **Language.** His line is answered by a Spanish-language service. His English on call 3
   is clear and workable. Pete's customers are in their homes — worth confirming with Pete
   that this is a non-issue rather than assuming it.
